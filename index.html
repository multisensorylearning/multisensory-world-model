Multisensory Continual Learning Adapting Pretrained Visuomotor Policies to Force

Abstract

Robot manipulation often depends on sensory data beyond vision, especially in contact-rich tasks where force, tactile, or audio feedback reveals interaction states not directly visible from images. Yet these modalities are hardware- and task-specific, and large multisensory datasets remain scarce, making it impractical to pretrain policies with every sensor they may encounter.

We study multisensory continual learning: adapting a pretrained robot policy to new tasks with newly introduced modalities while preserving performance under the original sensor suite. We propose MuSe, which integrates limited multisensory data into pretrained vision-action policies using token-level multi-stage fusion, multisensory future prediction, and episodic recall on pretraining data.

Instantiated with force-torque sensing, MuSe improves contact-rich finetuning tasks, predicts force on pretraining tasks where force supervision was never used, and improves performance on original pretraining tasks after multisensory finetuning.

Method

Multi-stage fusion

Force histories are encoded into the same token space as images, proprioception, language, and actions for early transformer-level interaction, then injected again through late cross-attention adapters.

Multisensory future prediction

The model predicts future actions, future visual observations, and future F/T signals, encouraging a shared representation grounded in both visual dynamics and contact.

Episodic recall

Finetuning mixes new multisensory data with the original pretraining data. Missing force inputs are masked, preserving old skills while adding the new modality.

Why F/T?

Force-torque sensing exposes the physical interaction state that images often hide: contact onset, slippage, jamming, excessive normal force, and surface compliance.

That makes F/T especially useful for wiping, where success depends on applying enough force without exceeding safety limits, and peg insertion, where the robot must detect jams and adjust rather than pushing blindly. The surprising result is broader: with the right objective and replay, limited force-labeled data can improve the original vision-only task distribution too.
